Starting from the computational challenges of spacetime Gaussian splatting, investigate whether jointly designing a camera formulation and a new dynamic-world representation can produce a particularly natural, compact, and fast differentiable rasterizer. Gaussian splatting is the concrete starting point and performance baseline, not a required architecture. You may improve it, generalize it, or replace its primitives and rendering law entirely. A central hypothesis is that representing camera-relative geometry through gauges, local frames, or a related geometric structure could expose shared computation across time. Take this hypothesis seriously, but allow it to fail. The target is a small mathematical object with an efficient evaluation and adjoint, not an elaborate vocabulary around conventional rendering.

Formalize the problem. Let θ describe one persistent dynamic scene, γ(t) a camera trajectory, and R(θ, γ(t), t) the rendered image. For T queried times over a fixed physical interval, seek reusable structure in both forward evaluation and gradients with respect to scene parameters and, where applicable, camera parameters. Define exactly which costs can grow sublinearly in T. Materializing T images with P pixels requires Ω(TP) output work; account for this explicitly rather than hiding it. Separate sample density from physical duration, scene complexity, motion complexity, resolution, and approximation tolerance. Account for preprocessing, compilation, visibility events, repairs, fallback, retained state, and the complete backward pass. State when event complexity itself grows with T or with the demanded accuracy.

Explore five substantively different directions. Keep one close to spacetime Gaussian splatting as a practical control; let the other four depart freely from the splat paradigm. Include a serious camera-gauge/world-representation co-design and a strong alternative that does not require gauge machinery. Choose constructions based on mathematical advantages rather than making five variations of one favored representation. Permit branches to be rejected, replaced, or combined when there is a specific mathematical reason, but do not end by combining every idea into one system.

For each direction, develop the following:

1. Representation and observables. Define the primitive or field, its parameters, support, evolution, camera measurement map, and rendering law. Explain what persists through time. Compare transported positions/support against changing density, changing amplitude, and deformation. Derive which degrees of freedom represent actual motion and which merely change its description. Favor moving persistent geometry where justified, but do not assume constant volume, rigidity, or density conservation without showing that the assumption fits the object being modeled. Keep scene state independent of the query camera; camera-dependent coordinate lowering can occur inside the renderer if it preserves the same physical scene.

2. Geometric structure. If using gauges, define the space on which transformations act, the transformation group or admissible maps, and the physical observables they preserve. Distinguish moving the physical camera from changing world coordinates, reparameterizing ray depth, or reparameterizing time. Derive the coupled transformations of scene and camera. If connections, covariant derivatives, parallel transport, or curvature are useful, define them and identify the computation or constraint each improves. Do not introduce them solely by analogy with physics. Invariance alone cannot eliminate physical screen motion or visibility events.

3. Closure and approximation. Determine whether the representation remains in the same family under its proposed transformations, temporal evolution, projection, and integration. Prove closure when it holds. Otherwise derive a controlled approximation, identify its validity domain, and explain how error affects support and visibility. Transform integration measures, appearance directions, derivatives, and regularizers consistently. Do not assume that a time-dependent rigid frame change preserves a joint spacetime Gaussian or a constant-velocity primitive.

4. Forward rasterization. Derive the actual operations required to generate pixels. Explain spatial support, visibility, occlusion, compositing or integration, and temporal sharing. Distinguish surface rasterization, ordered alpha compositing, and volume transport rather than silently treating their rendering laws as equivalent. Show what is computed once, what is updated at events, and what must still be evaluated at every sample. Include a worked scene where the proposed structure produces a concrete simplification and a counterexample where it does not.

5. Differentiation. Derive gradients or vector–Jacobian products for the proposed parameters. Separate derivatives inside fixed visibility/support configurations from derivatives at visibility changes or moving boundaries. Explain whether temporal contributions can be accumulated before applying a shared adjoint. Bound gradient error where possible; small image error alone is not sufficient. When representations use different parameters, compare derivative correctness against each representation's own reference, or map both to shared physical perturbations; do not demand equality of unrelated raw parameter gradients.

6. Complexity and falsification. State assumptions and derive time and memory bounds, including dependence on scene size, time samples, pixel count, and event complexity. Compare against ordinary batching, caching, shared rigid transforms, and an appropriate existing renderer. Demonstrate why any gain comes from the proposed structure rather than an unfair baseline or changed error tolerance. Identify worst cases, numerical conditioning problems, and the smallest experiment that would invalidate the claim. Include coherent motion, a static world with a moving camera, independent motion, and heavy occlusion.

Write a long, mathematically substantive research manuscript—roughly 12–20 pages of content, with definitions, propositions, explicit derivations, proofs where available, and counterexamples. Spend multiple pages developing each viable branch and deepen the strongest one or two. A decisive impossibility argument may replace a long treatment of a rejected branch; reallocate that space to promising alternatives. Clearly label proof gaps and conjectures, and do not claim experiments that were not run. Use primary literature to distinguish established machinery from potentially new contributions, and verify citations when browsing is available. End with a comparison table, one preferred representation–renderer pair, its remaining mathematical obstacles, and a minimal implementation and experiment plan. The desired result is a clean geometric construction whose computational advantage can be explained and tested.
